# Leadership, Communication & Coaching: Quincy Miller

## Overview / Summary

The engineering work gets the headlines, but the pattern underneath my career is communication under constraints: teaching, coaching, mentoring, and being the person in the room who can explain the technical thing to the person it affects. This document collects that evidence in one place.

## Adaptive Rowing Coaching (the story I would tell first)

In the Fall 2024 season I served as a volunteer coach and rowing partner for Scout, a sixteen-year-old athlete who has been blind since birth, through Coventry Lake Community Rowing. It was her first competitive season. I practiced with her three days a week, adapting technique instruction for an athlete who cannot watch a demonstration: precise, descriptive verbal cues, non-visual communication systems for on-water navigation, and trust built deliberately, because she was relying on my voice for her safety.

Our season culminated in competing together in the PR3 Inclusion Doubles at the Head of the Charles Regatta, where we finished with a time of 26:53. It remains one of the most rewarding things I have done. Professionally, it is also the hardest communication problem I have solved: explaining a physical skill to someone who processes the world differently than you do is the same skill as explaining a technical system to a stakeholder who does not share your context, executed under much higher stakes.

Rowing runs in the family: my mother is our community's crew coach and was my high school coach, and I have helped her build the program for years. I captained my high school crew team in 2019-2020.

## Teaching Engineers

*   **AI development workshop at Hubbell:** I designed and ran a hands-on workshop for professional engineers on GitHub Copilot best practices and prompt engineering, at a time when the team was still deciding how AI-assisted development fit their security posture. I also built the secure Python MCP servers that put documentation access inside developers' editors. The practices stuck after I left.
*   **Mentoring across the experience spectrum:** At Hubbell I onboarded and mentored a senior developer with seventeen years of experience who was new to the project, covering architecture, Databricks workflows, and domain knowledge. Mentoring someone far more experienced than you teaches a specific lesson: authority comes from context, not seniority, and respect comes from how you share it.
*   **Student developers at UConn:** As I finished my time at UConn Internal Insights & Innovation, I trained and onboarded four new student developers and transitioned every project I owned, with documentation, so the work survived me.
*   **Husky Developers Club:** Member, then Outreach Chair (2022-2023). I led workshops, consulted on student projects, and drove member growth for UConn's student software development club.

## Leading Teams

*   **Senior design lead:** I led a team of five engineers through two semesters and the full development lifecycle for the Multimodal Care Coordinator, facilitating the Agile ceremonies, coordinating with the sponsoring company's leadership, and taking the project to third place at the UConn Capstone Competition.
*   **Product ownership at Invested:** Running product and engineering means the leadership work is daily and unglamorous: deciding what not to build, giving customer feedback a path into the roadmap, and writing decisions down so they survive me.
*   **Earlier evidence of the pattern:** I was varsity captain of my high school wrestling team, and captain of my high school crew team. Being handed responsibility for a team's performance early shaped how I show up in every group since.

## Communication Artifacts

The most concrete evidence of how I communicate is what I write: architecture decision records across every codebase I own (thirty-four for AltIndex, thirteen for SponsorBridge, thirteen for Karrow), onboarding guides, and runbooks. I write decisions down with the rejected options included, because the reasoning is the valuable part, and because a decision you cannot explain is a decision you have not actually made.

## How This Shows Up at Work

*   I am used to being the engineer in the room with the people whose workflow is about to change, and used to being told my design is wrong by someone who has run the process for years. That correction is worth more than any spec.
*   I default to plain language over jargon when the audience is mixed, and to precision when it is technical.
*   I treat teaching as part of the job: a system only I can operate is a liability, not job security.
